ADDED — DEPTH OF UNDERSTANDING

Your task also includes discovering the relevant sides of each
concept BEFORE preparing it for visual generation.

Do not stop at describing what happens in one normal example.
Identify what the learner needs to understand about:
- why it happens;
- what happens behind the visible result;
- the conditions under which it happens;
- what changes when those conditions change;
- its development, consequences, and limitations;
- how the learner can investigate these relationships.

Keep one primary learning objective per Section.
Explore multiple relevant sides of that same objective.
Do not turn a Section into an unrelated collection of topics.

“All relevant sides” means the perspectives needed to explain,
use, or distinguish the concept correctly at the current learner
level. It does not mean every possible connection to the subject.

Preserve all original educational content. Add depth without
replacing explanations, equations, examples, or qualifications.

ADDED — FIRST PERSPECTIVE: TIME

For EVERY Section, examine TIME FIRST.

Determine whether the concept involves:
- an initial condition or something that must already exist;
- an event, operation, or change;
- intermediate states or hidden transitions;
- an immediate result;
- delayed consequences;
- repetition, accumulation, depletion, or repeated use;
- an ending, reset, recovery, release, or irreversible change.

Select only the relevant items. Do not force every concept into
a beginning-to-end lifecycle.

Distinguish clearly between:
1. Physical elapsed time.
2. Logical sequence or execution order.
3. Repetition count.
4. Animation playback time used only for presentation.

Do not treat these as interchangeable.

Ask:
- What would the learner miss by seeing only the initial and final states?
- Does the result depend on how long something happens?
- Does repeating the same action change the result?
- Does changing a condition partway through change what happens next?
- Is the change reversible, and under what conditions?

Use only source-supported answers.

If time is not relevant to the concept, explicitly state:
“Time is not a governing dimension of this concept.”
Then choose a more appropriate perspective, such as structure,
comparison, representation, or logical dependency.

Never invent durations, rates, intermediate mechanisms, or
long-term outcomes. Use qualitative progression if exact timing
is unsupported, and label it accordingly.

ADDED — DISCOVER THE CONCEPT'S CUSTOM PERSPECTIVES

After examining time, identify the OTHER perspectives that this
specific concept needs.

Use the following as discovery questions, not as mandatory views:

- Hidden mechanism:
  What internal process or relationship explains the visible result?

- Conditions and dependencies:
  What must be present, true, connected, or available?

- Change and intervention:
  What happens if a relevant input, condition, or action changes?
  What happens if a required action is omitted?

- Scale and repetition:
  Does behavior differ for one instance, many instances, or
  repeated operations?

- Boundaries and exceptions:
  When does the explanation stop applying?
  Which valid contrasting case prevents overgeneralization?

- Representation:
  How does the same underlying state appear as a physical view,
  diagram, code, equation, graph, output, or other useful representation?

- Structure and relationships:
  How do arrangement, membership, connections, or dependencies
  explain the concept?

- Application and consequence:
  What does this concept enable, prevent, consume, produce, or affect?

Discover additional perspectives when the concept requires them.
Name perspectives specifically for the concept; avoid generic
labels that do not explain what the learner will discover.

Do not automatically apply memory, chemistry, mechanical,
microscopic, or any other domain-specific perspective to
unrelated subjects.

For each candidate perspective, identify:
- the learner's question;
- its relationship to the primary learning objective;
- the source-supported explanation;
- what the learner should observe or compare;
- whether it is essential, supporting, not relevant, or unsupported.

Fully develop essential perspectives.
Include supporting perspectives only when they improve understanding.
Briefly identify important unsupported perspectives as evidence gaps.
Do not fill the output with irrelevant perspectives.

==================================================
ADDED — SOURCE FIDELITY AND EDUCATIONAL BOUNDARIES

Use the supplied educational material as the factual authority.

Distinguish:
- facts explicitly supported by the material;
- deductions from stated rules, identifying the rule and assumptions;
- illustrative examples or analogies, clearly labeled;
- missing information that the material does not establish.

Do not invent facts, equations, causal mechanisms, exceptions,
numerical thresholds, or citations to complete a perspective.

Use available source references for added factual explanations.
Never fabricate page numbers or source locations.

If a necessary perspective is not supported, state what is missing
and which explanation or behavior cannot yet be specified.

A qualitative model still requires a supported relationship.
“Qualitative” is not permission to invent how something behaves.

Respect the current lesson boundary and learner level.
If a perspective requires a separate advanced topic, identify it
as a related extension rather than silently expanding the lesson.

ADDED — CLARIFICATION OF ORIGINAL RULES

The original rules remain in place with these added requirements:

1. “OBJECTS and ACTIONS only” governs concrete scene description.
   Also include the rules, conditions, state relationships, and
   concise scientific constraints needed to make those actions
   correct. Do not omit them to achieve brevity.

2. “Optional user interaction” means a particular control type
   is optional. Meaningful learner investigation is REQUIRED
   when supported by the prepared content.
   Choose the appropriate interaction rather than automatically
   adding a slider, toggle, or time control.

3. “Final visual state” must provide inspectable evidence of the
   learning outcome. Where alternative conditions are supported,
   define the corresponding alternative outcomes as well.
   Do not force every learner action toward the same ending.

4. “No teaching explanations” excludes separate explanatory prose
   outside the generated prompts. It does not exclude necessary
   labels, concise feedback, scientific rules, or implementation
   constraints inside a prompt.

5. “No repetition” excludes unnecessary duplication within a
   prompt. Repeat essential context between independent prompts
   when each needs it to work on its own.

For each prepared investigation, explicitly describe:

Learner action →
affected state or relationship →
governing rule →
visible consequence →
evidence supporting the learning outcome.

Include supported baseline and contrasting conditions.

Require controls to affect the actual modeled state or the

Navigation, playback, camera movement, and visual decoration
alone do not count as meaningful investigation.

Require all linked representations to agree:
objects, labels, graphs, code, equations, outputs, and meters
must reflect the same case and state where applicable.

Include reset for state-changing investigations.
Include pause and inspection when progression carries meaning.
Reset returns to an initial condition; it must not imply that
a real irreversible process can physically run backward.

For noncausal concepts, use meaningful construction, comparison,
classification, or inspection without inventing causation.
